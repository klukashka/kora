# Own scheduler vs existing engine + session adapter: verdict

Status: desk analysis, 2026-10-15. Method: three independent reviews of kora's requirements (`docs/requirements/functional.md`, `docs/requirements/non-functional.md`) against the official documentation of one engine each, modelling kora as a thin adapter on top of it. No prototypes were built. Per-engine detail with requirement-by-requirement classification and sources: [temporal.md](temporal.md), [kestra.md](kestra.md), [airflow.md](airflow.md), [dbos.md](dbos.md) (added 2026-10-16). Amended 2026-10-16 (issue #22, after the [architecture critique of 2026-10-16](../../reviews/architecture-critique-2026-10-16.md#how-to-decide-about-the-own-scheduler)): the Temporal submission row corrected to at-least-once activities, the Airflow IR row corrected to the DAG processor, the attempt blocker reworded, a DBOS column added.

Hypothesis under test (from `docs/positioning.md`, "Evidence, hypotheses and open risks"): *an own scheduler is necessary; an existing engine plus a session adapter would not suffice.*

## What is engine-neutral

Every review found the same set of work that kora must write regardless of engine, because no engine has these objects: the Python DSL and YAML IR with layered presets; the artifact model (`StructuredOutput`, `VcsRef`, `AgentSession`, `CommandResult`, `ReviewBundle`) with per-attempt retention and lineage; the harness adapters (JSONL streaming, `max_events`, soft budget, usage completeness); the VCS, runtime, secrets and approval-channel protocols; the cost ledger and price table; the CLI and the approvals page. This is the larger part of kora by volume, and it is the part that differentiates it.

## What an engine would give for free

Temporal: durable timers and waits that hold no worker, retry policy fields that map 1:1 onto FR-6.3, heartbeats and stall detection, cancellation, child-workflow lifecycle, workflow-id dedupe for triggers, schedules, namespaces, per-queue runtime routing, audit history, worker versioning, Prometheus/OTel, and the entire NFR-1 scale target. Airflow: triggers, trigger rules, dynamic mapping, `awaiting_input` HITL, deferrable waits, AIP-105 retry policies, pools, DAG versioning, secrets backends, OTel/OpenLineage, REST v2, UI and CLI. Kestra: scheduler, triggers, `Pause` with typed `onResume`, subflow retry-as-new-execution, loops, concurrency, revisions, internal storage, Process/Docker runners, API and SDKs. DBOS: durable workflow state and restart recovery, durable sleep, persisted messages and events, workflow-id idempotency, queues with concurrency, rate limits, deduplication, priority and partitions, cron with backfill, step retries with a predicate, workflow timeouts, cancel, resume and fork, OTel traces and logs, SQLite and PostgreSQL under the same code.

## Blockers in common

1. **No engine makes a previous attempt addressable as an input.** Kestra records task-run attempts (state, worker, log file) and Airflow keeps per-try logs, but no engine hands attempt N+1 the artifacts of attempt N with lineage; `previous_attempt` inputs, per-attempt artifacts, retry diffs, per-attempt cost and lineage live in kora's own tables under every option. With Kestra and Airflow this is a hard conflict with the engine's retry model; with Temporal it is a loop kora writes inside the workflow.
2. **Approvals bound to content** (commit SHA plus gate digests, invalidation on new push, cancellation of superseded approvals) exist nowhere; Temporal's Update validators can express them, Kestra and Airflow cannot without bypass risk.
3. **Standalone mode** (one process, SQLite, for development and for the single-engineer persona) is not available in production form on any engine.
4. **Enterprise runtime** (an in-house enterprise CI and execution platform with a non-git VCS) is reachable only through adapter code in all three; Kestra additionally gates routing behind EE.

## Verdict

Kestra and Airflow fail the test in the direction the hypothesis predicted: the limitation list is long, and the result would be kora's control plane *beside* the engine, with higher operational cost and a Java or Python-file-based plugin surface that conflicts with the IR-first design. Both remain integration targets (trigger kora from them, expose a single-node operator/task), and Airflow's primitives (trigger rules, XCom vs state-store split, `awaiting_input`, AIP-105, `client_defaults` precedence) are worth borrowing.

Temporal is one of two credible alternatives. Its blockers are operational, not semantic: a mandatory Temporal deployment with no production single-process mode, a second source of truth (Temporal history for run state, kora DB for artifacts, cost, approvals and registry) that must be reconciled, a determinism and versioning discipline on kora's interpreter, and at target scale a Temporal Cloud bill in the low thousands of dollars per month or a self-hosted cluster. In exchange it removes roughly a third of the reliability and scheduling work and all of the scale work.

DBOS, assessed on 2026-10-16 ([dbos.md](dbos.md)), is the engine closest to kora's deployment shape: no service beyond kora's processes, the same PostgreSQL, SQLite in standalone. It would replace the scheduler's passes with a deterministic interpreter workflow over durable queues but, like Temporal, leaves kora's state model, the runtime protocol and the worker boundary in place, keeps at-least-once steps and two stores to reconcile, and adds a versioning rule under which a run finishes on the application version that started it; recovery across several processes is its paid control plane or coordination kora writes. It is not adopted before the first vertical slice and is re-evaluated when the slice has data.

The hypothesis is therefore **partly confirmed**: an own scheduler is not strictly necessary, but no engine removes kora's state model, and only Temporal and DBOS avoid fighting kora's retry and approval semantics. The decision reduces to one trade: *write a scheduler and state model* versus *operate Temporal, keep two stores in sync, and give up standalone mode*, or *embed DBOS, keep standalone mode, and accept its recovery and versioning rules*. The owner decides; the recommendation of this analysis is to keep the own control plane for MVP, design the scheduler's run-state model so that a Temporal-backed execution backend remains possible later (run state as an interpretable IR, attempts as explicit objects, waits as rows), and ship Airflow and Kestra integrations rather than cores.
